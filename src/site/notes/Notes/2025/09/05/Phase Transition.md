# Phase Transition

## 1. Fundamental Concepts

A phase transition describes the fundamental change in the state of a thermodynamic system. This process is governed by the interplay between different phases of matter under varying physical conditions.

### Phase
A **phase** is a region within a system characterized by uniform physical and chemical properties. It represents a distinct form of matter that is physically separable from other phases.
-   **Common Examples**: Solid, liquid, gas, and plasma.

### Phase Transition
A **phase transition** is the transformation of a system from one phase to another. This change is typically driven by the transfer of heat or a change in external conditions like pressure.

### Phase Boundary
**Phase boundaries** are the well-defined interfaces that separate distinct phases. At these boundaries, the system can exist in a state of equilibrium between the adjacent phases.

## 2. Common Types of Phase Transitions

Phase transitions are commonly observed and are named based on the initial and final states of matter:
-   **Melting**: The transition from a solid to a liquid.
-   **Freezing**: The transition from a liquid to a solid.
-   **Vaporization (Boiling)**: The transition from a liquid to a gas.
-   **Condensation**: The transition from a gas to a liquid.
-   **Sublimation**: The transition from a solid directly to a gas.
-   **Deposition**: The transition from a gas directly to a solid.

## 3. Thermodynamic Properties

Phase transitions are fundamentally thermodynamic phenomena, characterized by distinct changes in a system's energy and order.

-   **Enthalpy and Entropy**: Every phase transition is accompanied by a change in the system's **enthalpy** ($\Delta H$) and **entropy** ($\Delta S$).
-   **Latent Heat**: The **latent heat** ($L$) is the specific quantity of energy absorbed or released at a constant temperature during a phase transition. It is equivalent to the change in enthalpy, $L = \Delta H$.

## 4. Phase Diagrams

A **phase diagram** is a graphical tool used to visualize the conditions of temperature and pressure at which different phases of a substance are thermodynamically stable. The lines on the diagram represent the phase boundaries where transitions occur.

### The Triple Point
The **triple point** represents the unique set of temperature and pressure conditions at which the solid, liquid, and gas phases of a substance can coexist in thermodynamic equilibrium.

### The Critical Point
The **critical point** is the endpoint of the phase boundary between the liquid and gas phases.
-   **Supercritical Fluid**: Beyond the critical point, the distinction between liquid and gas disappears. The substance exists in a single phase known as a **supercritical fluid**, which has properties of both a liquid and a gas.

## 5. Classification of Phase Transitions

Phase transitions are formally classified according to the behavior of the system's free energy and its derivatives at the transition point.

### First-Order Phase Transitions
A **first-order phase transition** is characterized by a discontinuity in the first derivatives of the free energy (e.g., entropy and volume).
-   **Latent Heat**: These transitions involve a non-zero latent heat ($L \neq 0$).
-   **Discontinuous Properties**: The system experiences an abrupt change in density, entropy, and other first-order properties.
-   **Examples**: Common transitions like melting, boiling, and sublimation fall into this category.

### Second-Order Phase Transitions
Also known as *continuous phase transitions*, **second-order phase transitions** are characterized by continuous first derivatives of the free energy, but a discontinuity or divergence in the second derivatives (e.g., heat capacity).
-   **Latent Heat**: These transitions have zero latent heat ($L = 0$).
-   **Continuous Properties**: Entropy and volume change continuously across the transition point.
-   **Divergent Properties**: The heat capacity often shows a sharp peak or diverges at the transition temperature.
-   **Examples**: The transition from ferromagnetism to paramagnetism at the Curie temperature and the onset of superconductivity. The [[Notes/2025/09/05/Ising Model\|Ising Model]] provides a theoretical framework for studying such transitions.
